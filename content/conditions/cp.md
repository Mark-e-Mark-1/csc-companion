## Overview

Stub for medical team review. Cerebral palsy is a group of disorders of movement and posture from a non-progressive injury to the developing brain. Severity, type, and associated conditions (pain, epilepsy, vision, hearing, communication, learning) vary more than the label suggests. Intellectual disability is not part of the definition. Many children have typical learning. Some do not. Ask. PM&R, PT, OT, and speech are usually already involved.

What the medical team still needs to add: how your clinic talks about gross motor function levels, spasticity treatments, and pain.

## Key treatments

Physical and occupational therapy, braces, wheelchairs and other equipment, speech and AAC, medicines or injections for tone, orthopedic or neurosurgical procedures for some children, and treatment of associated epilepsy or reflux. CSC does not set the equipment or the tone plan.

## Treatment burden

Appointments, stretching programs, equipment that does not fit the house or the school, and a caregiver body that is also getting injured from transfers. The child is often spoken about in the room. That is its own burden.

## Adherence

Home exercise fails when it hurts, when it takes the whole evening, or when the child has no say. Equipment is abandoned when it is uncomfortable or babyish. Ask which piece actually helps and which piece is a fight.

## Mental health and development

Pain, fatigue, sleep, and being left out of peer activities affect mood. Communication differences are misread as low understanding or as behavior. Always assess how the child communicates before you interpret a yes. Adolescence brings privacy, dating, and the insult of still being helped with private care by a parent. Include the child in that conversation.

## Long-term effects

CP itself is not progressive, and the musculoskeletal consequences can worsen over time: contractures, pain, hip problems, fatigue. Adult services are a cliff. Mental health risk follows pain and exclusion more than the motor label alone.

## Red flags

- New severe pain, a change in walking or transfers, trouble breathing, or a seizure pattern that is new. Medical team.
- Feeding or swallowing safety: coughing with meals, recurrent chest infections. Speech and the medical team, not a behavior plan about "eating fast."
- Self-injury, suicidal ideation, or a caregiver who cannot safely transfer and has no backup.
- Skin breakdown from a chair or a brace.

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Give the communication method time. Talk to the child, not only the adult. Goals might be pain coping, peer scripts, or a say in the daily care. Do not make walking the psychological goal if the child uses a wheelchair and is fine with that.

### Family

Caregiver injury and grief are in the room. So is pride. Help adults stop finishing the child's sentences if the child has a way to answer. Siblings need a place that is not only the therapy schedule.

### Parent training

Coach one care routine that offers a real choice. Praise communication attempts. Keep behavior plans physically possible. A demand the body cannot meet is not noncompliance.

## Intake questions

- How does the child communicate, and how long do adults wait?
- Where is pain, and what is the current tone or equipment plan?
- What can the child do that adults still do out of habit?
- Who is at risk of injury during care tasks?

## Differentials

- Behavior versus pain, reflux, constipation, or fatigue.
- "Not trying" versus a motor plan that takes longer than the adult allowed.
- Low mood versus social exclusion and lack of a communication system that works with peers.
- Request for high-hour ABA versus a CSC parent-training need. Many-hour ABA is outside scope.

## Modifications

- Accessible room, time for transfers, and the child's own communication system.
- Do not use motor goals PT has not set.
- Speak to the child at chronological age.
- Use the developmental-complexity training for adaptation, not as a substitute for rehab.
