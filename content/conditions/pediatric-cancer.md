## Overview

Childhood cancer is a group of diseases, not one illness with one script. This card covers the two the team asked for in draft: leukemia, especially acute lymphoblastic leukemia, the most common childhood cancer, and brain tumors. Other diagnoses need oncology to tell you what is different. Do not stretch this card to cover a diagnosis you have not asked about.

Active treatment, maintenance, the months after treatment, and long-term survivorship are different psychological jobs. Relapse is another. Prognosis varies enormously. Do not reassure a family with a cure rate you heard somewhere, and do not tell a brain-tumor family that "children's cancer usually turns out fine." Use the words oncology is already using.

Psychosocial care is a standard of pediatric oncology, not a luxury if there is time. The Standards of Care for children with cancer and their families describe screening, access to psychology and psychiatry, school support, and palliative care as expected. CSC works beside the oncology psychosocial clinicians. Ask who is already involved so the child does not get two competing plans.

You do not manage chemotherapy, radiation, fever rules, or transfusions. You do need to know enough to stop psychologizing a medical emergency, a steroid pulse, or a late effect.

## Key treatments

### Leukemia

Treatment is a long protocol: induction, consolidation, and maintenance that can run for years, built by the oncology group. A central line or port is common. Intrathecal chemotherapy puts medicine into the spinal fluid to protect the brain. Cranial radiation is much less common in modern leukemia therapy than in older eras. Do not assume this child had it. Ask.

Corticosteroids, often dexamethasone or prednisone, are given in pulses. Those weeks are a known behavioral risk. Vincristine can cause neuropathy and constipation. Asparaginase and other agents have their own medical toxicities. You do not need to recite the protocol. You need the calendar: what is this child receiving this month, and what did the team say it does to mood, sleep, walking, and school.

### Brain tumors

Treatment may include surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, or a combination, depending on the tumor. Deficits follow location. Posterior fossa surgery can be followed by cerebellar mutism, also called posterior fossa syndrome: reduced speech, emotional lability, and motor changes. Families are sometimes told it will simply resolve. Recovery is variable. Do not treat the speech change as selective mutism or as oppositionality.

Cranial radiation and some chemotherapies carry cognitive, endocrine, and hearing late effects. Younger age at cranial radiation is associated with greater cognitive risk. You do not need the dose in your head to take that seriously. Neuropsychology and oncology interpret the dose. You adapt the therapy hour and the school plan to the result.

### Steroids, chemotherapy, and radiation

Steroid pulses can bring irritability, mood swings, sleep loss, a fierce appetite, tearfulness, and aggression. Less often, severe agitation or psychotic symptoms appear. These are medicine effects. Time-limited does not mean trivial. Plan for the pulse weeks. Tell the school. Tell the family it is the medicine. A new severe psychiatric picture goes to oncology the same day, not to a new diagnosis you make alone.

Chemotherapy late effects that change therapy include fatigue, hearing loss (especially after platinum agents — ask audiology), neuropathy, heart-muscle effects after anthracyclines (activity limits are medical), and cognitive effects after intrathecal methotrexate. Radiation late effects include cognitive change, endocrine problems (growth, puberty, thyroid), and second-cancer risk, all followed by the survivorship team.

School is a treatment setting. Hospital-homebound instruction, a 504 plan or an IEP, infection limits during active therapy, and a neuropsychology evaluation after brain tumor treatment or cranial radiation are ordinary tools. Processing speed that looks like "not trying" is a classic miss.

## Treatment burden

Active treatment reorganizes the household: appointments, admissions, a parent sleeping at the bedside, siblings farmed out, jobs at risk, infection rules that shrink the world. Maintenance looks quieter and is still years of pills, pulses, and fear of fever. Survivorship looks "done" to relatives and is not done to the family, who now watch for late effects and for relapse.

Procedures, port access, and lumbar punctures are repeated. A child who "should be used to it" is often a child who has learned to freeze or to fight. Caregivers carry traumatic stress of their own. Financial toxicity is part of the history. Ask, without turning the session into a benefits interview you cannot finish.

Guilt shows up as overprotection, as extra gifts, and as an inability to set an ordinary limit. The child may also feel guilty, especially younger children, who invent a cause.

## Adherence

During active therapy the medical team drives adherence, and the stakes of a missed medicine are theirs to explain. Your role is barriers: taste, nausea, a fight at pill time, a teenager who is done being a patient, a caregiver who cannot afford to miss another shift and misses a dose.

Pill swallowing is a skill that can be taught. Steroid weeks are a bad time to introduce a new behavioral demand. Maintenance fatigue is real: the child looks well, the end is not here, and the family loosens. Do not moralize that. Look at the calendar with them.

After treatment, follow-up visits and late-effect screening become the adherence task. Avoidance is often fear of hearing bad news. Name the fear and problem-solve the appointment. Do not call it denial as an insult.

## Mental health and development

Worry, procedural fear, low mood, traumatic stress, and family strain are common and do not all need a psychiatric diagnosis. Some do. Screen. Caregiver traumatic stress and sibling distress are part of the child's treatment, because the house is the recovery environment.

Young children may show the illness in play, sleep, clinginess, and regression. They need the truth in short, concrete words. Secrets breed scarier inventions. School-age children track fairness, missed work, friends who moved on, and whether they look different. Hair loss, a feeding tube, or steroids' effect on the face are social injuries. Adolescents are trying to be ordinary while the body and the calendar refuse. Privacy, dating, fertility questions, and driving sit beside scan anxiety.

Fertility counseling is the medical team's. Adolescents may be holding it unspoken. You can ask whether anyone has talked with them about the future of their body, and then route the facts back to oncology rather than improvising.

Steroid weeks in a young child look like a behavior disorder. In a teenager they look like a mood disorder or like attitude. Check the pulse calendar before you change the diagnosis.

Brain tumor survivors may have attention, processing-speed, working-memory, and executive-function difficulties, plus fatigue, hearing or vision changes, and endocrine late effects. Social skills can lag because of missed years, cognitive change, or both. Adapt the session. Refer for neuropsychology rather than repeating a talk therapy that assumes typical processing.

## Long-term effects

The Children's Oncology Group long-term follow-up guidelines are the map survivorship clinics use. Late effects depend on the tumor, the age, the drugs, the radiation field, and the doses. You will not memorize that grid. You will ask whether a survivorship or neuropsychology visit has happened, and you will read the recommendations that change school and daily demands.

Cognitive late effects can appear or become more obvious years later, as school demands rise. A child who looked fine in second grade may struggle in fifth. That is not a surprise to oncology. It is often a surprise to the school.

Heart, hearing, endocrine, growth, and second-cancer surveillance stay with the medical team. Activity restrictions after anthracyclines are not yours to loosen because the child is sad about sitting out. Grief for a lost version of the future, including after cure, is legitimate. So is fear before scans. People call that scanxiety. It is not irrational. It still may be treatable if it has taken over the month before every scan.

Palliative care can be involved early, alongside curative treatment. Their involvement does not mean everyone has given up. If you do not know why they are on the team, ask. Do not avoid the family because the word is frightening.

## Red flags

- Fever, especially if the family has been told the child may be neutropenic or immune-suppressed. Follow their thermometer rule. This is an emergency, not a morning appointment.
- New headache with vomiting, a seizure, new weakness, vision change, confusion, or a change in alertness. Emergency care.
- Bleeding, severe pain, trouble breathing, or a line that looks infected. Medical team now.
- Steroid pulse with severe agitation, mania, or psychotic symptoms. Oncology the same day.
- Suicidal ideation, self-harm, or a caregiver so depleted that medicines are not being given and the team does not know.
- A sudden cognitive or speech change from the child's baseline, including after brain surgery. Do not reframe posterior fossa syndrome as behavior.
- Disclosure of abuse or neglect, using your reporting duty. Illness in the family does not pause that duty.
- Any request to interpret a lab, a count, or a scan. Send it back to oncology.

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Teach procedural coping against the actual procedure: one voice, a choice that is real, a stop signal the nurse will honor if the procedure allows it. Confirm with the team which choices are real. A fake choice is worse than none.

Separate ordinary worry, scan-related fear, and traumatic memories of restraint or the ICU. Do not start trauma processing on an exhausted child in the middle of induction. Stabilize, predict, and give the caregiver a job.

Use the family's words for the illness. Do not install a battle metaphor they did not choose. Body image, hair, and the face changes of steroids deserve plain language and a plan for school re-entry, not a pep talk.

For brain tumor late effects, shorten the hour if fatigue demands it, slow your language, and put the plan in a picture or a short list. Advocate for school supports as a concrete task. Identity work can include the illness and must not require the child to be inspiring.

### Family

Ask what the child has been told, and help the adults align. Caregivers often disagree about how much to say. Caregiver traumatic stress, couple strain, and sibling resentment are in the work. Decision fatigue is real. Do not add a complicated home program during induction week.

Help the family keep one ordinary limit alive, so guilt does not erase every rule. Help them keep one ordinary pleasure alive too. Both are treatment.

If relatives are flooding the child with optimism or with stories of someone who died, problem-solve the doorway. The family is allowed to decide who visits.

### Parent training

During steroid pulses: lower demands, protect sleep, warn the school, and avoid new skill battles. The pulse will end. A behavior chart that punishes steroid irritability will fail and will shame the child.

Pill swallowing can be practiced in steps, with the team's pharmacy or nursing input if the medicine cannot be cut or mixed. Reinforce activity that matches real fatigue: pacing, not pushing through and not total withdrawal. Ask oncology or PT what the body is allowed to do.

For procedures, rehearse one caregiver script so the child does not hear three frightened adults. Comfort item, one small choice, one job for the child. Consistent limits on hitting during a pulse can be taught without a lecture about respect. The parent is allowed to be frightened and still hold a simple boundary.

After treatment, coach the return of age-appropriate expectations. Some families stay in hospital mode for years. Name that gently, and move one expectation at a time.

## Intake questions

- What is the diagnosis, where are they in treatment, and what medicines are on this month's calendar, especially steroids?
- What does the child know, and what words does the family use?
- Any new neurologic symptom, fever rule, or activity restriction you must not work around?
- What has school been told, and has neuropsychology been done or recommended?
- Who is depleted at home, and who is already providing psychosocial care on the oncology team?

## Differentials

- Irritability, aggression, or sleep collapse during a steroid pulse versus a new mood or behavior disorder. Check the calendar. Severe symptoms still go to oncology the same day.
- Inattention, slow work, or "not trying" at school versus processing-speed and working-memory late effects, fatigue, hearing loss, absence from instruction, or seizures. This differential is especially important after a brain tumor, cranial radiation, or intrathecal chemotherapy.
- Reduced speech and emotional lability after posterior fossa surgery versus selective mutism, depression, or oppositionality.
- Procedural fear versus a current medical emergency that looks similar: vomiting, headache, trouble breathing.
- Grief, ordinary sadness, and depression can all be present. Do not force cheerfulness, and do not miss suicidal ideation because "of course they are sad."
- Caregiver anxiety versus a fever rule the family was correctly taught to treat as an emergency. Do not desensitize a parent to a warning sign.

## Modifications

- Ask the calendar before you set homework. Steroid week and the days after a lumbar puncture are bad times for a new demand.
- Keep sessions shorter during active treatment if fatigue or infection risk says so. Offer caregiver-only time when the child is inpatient or asleep.
- Do not interpret labs or scans. Do not loosen an activity restriction.
- Use concrete, short plans for children with cognitive late effects. Repeat across visits. Write it down.
- Coordinate with the oncology social worker or psychologist so goals do not collide.
- For adolescents, offer time without the caregiver. Fertility, peers, and privacy may not come up with a parent in the chair.
