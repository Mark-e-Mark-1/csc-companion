## Overview

Stub for medical team review. Epilepsy is a tendency to unprovoked seizures. Type matters: absence, focal, generalized convulsive, and others do not look alike and do not disrupt school the same way. Neurology owns the diagnosis, the medicine, and the rescue plan. Some events that look neurologic are functional. That is a different card, and neurology should say which is which.

What the medical team still needs to add: the seizure types you want therapists to recognize, the local rescue-plan language, and the medicines most often tied to mood or irritability in your clinic.

## Key treatments

Daily antiseizure medicines, sometimes a diet, a device, or surgery. Rescue medicine for prolonged or clustered seizures, written down for school. Many children also need a sleep plan, because sleep loss lowers the threshold. You do not adjust medicines or tell a family to "give it one more minute" if their plan says otherwise.

## Treatment burden

Medicine twice a day, refills, levels, EEGs, and the background fear of the next seizure. Driving, swimming, and climbing restrictions hit adolescents hard. Caregivers may not sleep. School plans and who is allowed to give rescue medicine take real advocacy.

## Adherence

Missed doses cluster around adolescence, side effects, and a stretch without seizures that makes the medicine feel optional. A missed dose is a neurology safety issue. Ask which dose, and whether side effects are the reason.

## Mental health and development

ADHD symptoms, anxiety, depression, and learning problems are all more common, from the epilepsy, the medicine, the sleep, or a shared developmental condition. Staring spells can be absence seizures. Irritability can be a medicine effect. Some medicines, including levetiracetam for a subset of children, are associated with mood or behavior change. Do not guess which medicine. Ask neurology what they suspect, and do not stop it yourself.

## Long-term effects

Depends on syndrome, seizure control, and cause. Some epilepsies resolve. Some are part of a broader developmental condition. SUDEP is a real risk neurology discusses with families. Do not introduce it casually, and do not be shocked if a parent is carrying it.

## Red flags

- A seizure longer than the written rescue plan allows, clusters, trouble breathing, injury, or a first convulsion. Follow the plan and emergency services.
- A change in seizure pattern, a new type of spell, or medicine stopped suddenly.
- Suicidal ideation. Some antiseizure medicines carry mood warnings. Report timing to neurology and use your safety pathway.
- Events nobody has classified. Do not decide alone that they are "just behavior."

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Worry about the next seizure, shame after a public event, and missed school are legitimate targets. Keep ADHD and mood questions open while spells and medicines are being sorted. Give the child a short script for peers.

### Family

Caregiver hypervigilance is understandable and can squeeze independence. Expand one safe freedom the neurologist has already allowed. Align adults on the rescue plan so the child hears one version.

### Parent training

Rehearse the rescue plan until the adult can say it calmly. Praise ordinary rules that are not about seizures, so the whole relationship is not surveillance. Do not punish a post-ictal period of confusion or sleep.

## Intake questions

- What type of seizures, when was the last one, and what does the rescue plan say word for word?
- Any staring spells that school is calling inattention?
- Which medicine changed recently, and did mood, appetite, or sleep change with it?
- What activities are restricted, and which of those is the child breaking?

## Differentials

- ADHD versus absence seizures versus medication side effects versus sleep loss. This is the differential the additional-topics training is for. Do not close it in one interview.
- Irritability versus a named medicine effect versus pain versus a mood disorder.
- Functional events versus epileptic events. Neurology makes that call. A child can have both.

## Modifications

- Know the rescue plan before you do exposure work that could be mistaken for a spell, including breath-holding or intense crying practice.
- Do not suggest stopping a medicine because behavior improved or worsened.
- If spells are unclassified, pause the behavior plan and ask neurology.
- Link the ADHD-differential training when the URL is added.
