## Overview

Stub for medical team review. Functional neurological symptom disorder involves neurological symptoms — weakness, movements, sensory change, events that look like seizures — that are real and are not explained by a structural lesion of the kind the symptom suggests. Best practice is a positive diagnosis by neurology, not a diagnosis of exclusion announced as "nothing is wrong," and not an accusation of faking.

## Key treatments

A clear explanation from the diagnosing clinician, physiotherapy for motor symptoms, and psychological treatment such as CBT adapted for FND. Medicines may treat coexisting pain, anxiety, or depression. They do not "fix" FND by themselves. You should not be the first person to say the symptom is functional if neurology has not said it.

## Treatment burden

Families have often been through many tests and at least one clinician who implied the child was pretending. School has sometimes treated the symptom as truancy. The household organizes around the symptom. That is exhausting even when everyone believes the child.

## Adherence

Children and parents drop out when they hear "it's psychological" as a dismissal. Attendance improves when the explanation preserves the reality of the symptom and offers a mechanism they can respect. Ask what they were told, in their words, before you add yours.

## Mental health and development

Anxiety, depression, trauma, and perfectionism are common and are not required for the diagnosis. A child can have FND and epilepsy, or FND and a developmental difference. Younger children need a concrete explanation. Adolescents need dignity and a way back to school that is not "just go."

## Long-term effects

Many improve, especially with an early, respectful explanation and rehab. Some symptoms persist. Chronic disability, school exit, and family conflict are the risks of a slow or blaming pathway. Do not promise a timeline.

## Red flags

- New symptoms neurology has not seen: a new severe headache, true loss of consciousness with injury, fever, rapidly worsening weakness. Functional history does not cancel emergency care.
- Suicidal ideation or self-harm.
- A school or family plan that requires the child to prove the symptom is real by suffering.

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Use the neurologist's explanation. Add a practical goal: one step back toward walking, talking, or attending school. Attention to the symptom from adults often maintains it. Shift attention toward the function the child is recovering, without withdrawing warmth.

### Family

Parents are not the cause by default. They may be reinforcing the symptom by accident because they are scared. Teach a response that stays kind and stops organizing the whole day around the symptom. Screen for caregiver distress.

### Parent training

One calm response when the symptom appears. Avoid quizzes ("can you really not move?"). Avoid rewards that require the symptom. Praise the fragment of ordinary function. Loop PT in so the home practice matches theirs.

## Intake questions

- Who made the diagnosis, and what words did they use with the family?
- What happens in the ten seconds after the symptom starts, at home and at school?
- Is epilepsy also on the list, and is there a rescue plan you must not ignore?
- What has school been told to do?

## Differentials

- Functional events versus epileptic seizures. A child can have both. Rescue plans still apply to the epileptic ones.
- FND versus a neurological diagnosis that was missed. New or changing signs go back to neurology.
- Symptom attention versus malingering. Do not reach for faking. It is rarely the right word and it ends the treatment.

## Modifications

- Do not start by hunting a hidden trauma as the price of admission. Trauma may be present. It is not the only door.
- Coordinate with PT. Contradictory plans are common and harmful.
- Keep school goals small and agreed with the medical team.
