## Overview

Stub for medical team review. Spinal cord injury in a child or adolescent may be traumatic or related to a medical cause. The level and completeness of the injury decide movement, sensation, bowel and bladder, breathing, and blood-pressure control. PM&R owns that map. Do not generalize from one child to the next.

What the medical team still needs to add: a one-page autonomic dysreflexia script your clinic wants families to use, bowel and bladder basics you are allowed to know, and skin-check expectations.

## Key treatments

Rehabilitation therapies, equipment, bowel and bladder programs, spasticity treatment, and respiratory care when the level requires it. Psychological care for adjustment, trauma, pain, and family role change. You do not redesign the bladder program.

## Treatment burden

Care hours fall on families. Privacy disappears at an age when privacy matters most. School access, transportation, and a parent who can no longer work are daily facts. The child is often praised for inspiration and not asked what hurts.

## Adherence

Pressure reliefs, bowel programs, and skin checks fail when they are boring, when they require help the teen does not want, or when depression has flattened the day. A skipped pressure relief is a wound risk. Treat it as health behavior, not as attitude only.

## Mental health and development

Traumatic stress from the injury event, grief for the body that changed, depression, and pain are common. Children injured young may not remember a before. Adolescents do, and peers and dating become the sharp edge. Caregivers are exhausted and sometimes injured themselves. Developmental stage still applies: a 7-year-old and a 16-year-old do not need the same talk about the injury.

## Long-term effects

Depends on level. Breathing, autonomic function, fertility, pain, and overuse of the arms can all matter later. Transition to adult rehab is a known gap. Do not invent a prognosis. Ask PM&R what they have already told the family.

## Red flags

- Autonomic dysreflexia, especially with injuries at thoracic level T6 and above: sudden headache, flushing, sweating, goosebumps, a slow heart rate, anxiety, or a feeling that something is very wrong. This is an emergency. Sit the person up if that is the plan they were taught, loosen tight clothing, and get emergency care. Emptying the bladder or bowel is the team's algorithm, not something to improvise if you have not been trained. Do not treat this as a panic attack.
- Fever, a new pressure injury, trouble breathing, or a change in strength. Medical team.
- Suicidal ideation. Ask, including about equipment and access.
- A caregiver who cannot safely do transfers.

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Adjustment, trauma memories of the injury, peer scripts, and a concrete problem such as asking for help without losing the afternoon. Privacy and body image are legitimate entire goals. Do not rush meaning-making.

### Family

Role changes, caregiver health, and siblings. Parents may hover in ways that block the skills rehab is teaching. Align with the rehab goals rather than building a second plan.

### Parent training

Coach the care task that is becoming a fight, with the rehab team's steps. Keep one expectation that is not medical so the relationship is not only care. For younger children, the adult runs the safety routines. For teens, negotiate one piece of self-care they will own.

## Intake questions

- What level of injury has PM&R documented, and does the family have an autonomic dysreflexia plan?
- What do bowel, bladder, skin checks, and transfers look like, and where is the conflict?
- What changed in mood, sleep, pain, and school?
- Who else was affected by the event?

## Differentials

- Panic or irritability versus autonomic dysreflexia. Headache plus flushing is not a coping failure.
- Depression versus pain, poor sleep, or a bladder infection.
- Refusal of a care task versus pain, spasticity, or a task that has not been adapted to the child's hands.

## Modifications

- Accessible space, time, and no surprise physical demands.
- Know the dysreflexia plan before you do any exposure that could be mistaken for it, and before you coach "sit with the sensation" for a headache.
- Do not give bowel, bladder, or skin advice beyond what rehab taught.
- Use the developmental lens for age. The injury is not the whole developmental profile.
