## Overview

Stub for medical team review. Tic disorders include provisional tic disorder, persistent motor or vocal tic disorder, and Tourette syndrome. Tics are sudden, recurrent, non-rhythmic movements or sounds. They wax and wane. They are suggestible and they worsen with stress, illness, and sometimes with being watched. They are not a choice and not a defiance. The scope lists tic disorders under Path 1 because there is an evidence-based behavioral treatment.

The additional-topics training includes tics. Use it.

What the medical team still needs to add: when your clinic starts medicine, and how you want therapists to talk about sudden tic-like events that may not be typical tics.

## Key treatments

Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention for Tics (CBIT), including habit reversal, is the first-line behavioral treatment for many children when tics cause impairment. Psychoeducation alone helps some families. Medicines are a neurology or psychiatry decision when tics are impairing and behavioral treatment is not enough or not available. You do not start those medicines. Co-occurring ADHD, OCD, and anxiety are treated on their own merits. Treating the comorbidity often matters as much as treating the tic.

## Treatment burden

A child who is stared at, corrected, or sent out of class. A parent who cannot stop saying "stop." Hours of internet videos that made the tics worse. Siblings who imitate, sometimes cruelly, sometimes because tics are contagious in a room for a little while.

## Adherence

CBIT practice fails when it is long, when it is done as punishment, or when the competing response is embarrassing. Awareness training fails if the child is too young or too ashamed. Ask for two minutes of practice, not a personality change.

## Mental health and development

ADHD, OCD, anxiety, and learning problems commonly travel with Tourette syndrome. Rage attacks in some children are a known associated problem and are not ordinary oppositionality. Bullying and shame drive withdrawal. Younger children need caregiver-delivered CBIT components. School-age children can learn a competing response. Adolescents care about whether anyone can tell.

## Long-term effects

Many children's tics improve by late adolescence. Some do not. The comorbidities often outlast the worst tic years. Do not promise they will grow out of it on a date. Do not warn them that it is forever.

## Red flags

- Sudden severe neurological symptoms that are not this child's usual tics: prolonged unresponsiveness, new weakness, a seizure-like event. Neurology or emergency care. Not every flare is "just tics."
- Self-injury from a tic, such as hitting the face hard. Tell neurology. Protect the body.
- Suicidal ideation or bullying that has become unsafe.
- A family request to punish tics until they stop. That is not treatment. Explain why, and do not build the chart.

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Psychoeducation that removes blame. If you are trained in CBIT, use it for tics that bother the child, not for every tic an adult dislikes. If you are not trained, say so and use the recorded training plus supervision rather than inventing a suppression program. Treat the OCD, anxiety, or ADHD problem that is actually causing more impairment.

### Family

Take the correction out of the room. Parents reduce tic severity more by changing their response than by reminding. Siblings need a script. Schools need a short letter neurology is willing to stand behind.

### Parent training

No punishment for tics. No reward that requires tic-free hours, which the child cannot guarantee. Praise the competing response if you are using one. Ignore the tic the family agreed to stop commenting on. Coach the adult who comments the most.

## Intake questions

- Which tics, for how long, and which one actually impairs school or friends?
- What do adults do when the tic happens?
- Any ADHD, OCD, anxiety, or rage episodes?
- Has neurology seen the child, and is anyone asking you to medicate?

## Differentials

- Tics versus compulsions versus stereotypies versus seizures versus functional tic-like events. The treatment differs. Neurology helps when the phenomenology is unclear.
- ADHD treatment can sometimes affect tics. That is a prescriber observation, not a reason for you to stop a stimulant.
- "Behavior" in class versus a tic plus an adult correction cycle.

## Modifications

- Do not draw attention to the tic during the session unless you are doing a CBIT exercise the child agreed to.
- Do not treat a waxing week as failure of the child.
- Match CBIT demands to developmental level. A competing response is a motor skill. Break it down.
- Use the tics material in the additional-topics training.
