## Overview

Stub for medical team review. Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder is a pattern of limited eating with nutritional, growth, or psychosocial consequences, without the body-image fear that defines anorexia nervosa. Pediatric feeding disorders overlap and are often the better frame in young children, especially with skill, medical, or developmental contributors. Speech, GI, dietitian, and sometimes OT are already in the case. Do not start a food hierarchy that ignores them.

What the medical team still needs to add: your malnutrition thresholds, tube-feeding philosophy, and which foods or textures are off-limits until swallow safety is cleared.

## Key treatments

Medical stabilization and nutrition come first. Psychological treatment is often a form of CBT adapted for ARFID, family-based work, or caregiver coaching at meals, matched to age. Feeding therapy addresses skill and texture. Medicines are sometimes used for appetite or anxiety. The team chooses. You do not set calorie targets.

## Treatment burden

Meals become a battlefield. One caregiver is the "only one who can feed them." Family restaurants, holidays, and cultural foods drop out. Tube feeds, if present, bring relief and grief at the same time. Siblings eat differently and notice.

## Adherence

Pressure, bribes, and long sit-times usually make the range smaller. Families have been told opposite things by different clinicians. Ask what they were praised for and what they were scolded for. A plan that removes a culturally important food without asking will not be kept, and should not be written.

## Mental health and development

Anxiety, sensory sensitivity, autism, and GI pain are common companions. ARFID is not "picky eating" as an insult, and it is not a failure of parenting. Young children need caregiver-delivered interventions. School-age children can join a small ladder. Adolescents may also have, or be developing, body-image concerns. Reassess. Do not assume the diagnosis is frozen.

## Long-term effects

Growth faltering, micronutrient deficiency, low bone density, and, for some, lasting tube dependence or social exclusion around food. The dietitian tracks the body. You track fear, family meals, and function.

## Red flags

- Fainting, chest pain, dizziness, refusal of all fluids, rapid weight loss, or a caregiver report that the child has stopped drinking. Medical assessment now.
- Swallowing cough, choking, or a food texture speech has not cleared.
- Suicidal ideation or a coexisting body-image eating disorder with medical instability.
- Do not recommend a cleanse, an appetite supplement, or a fast.

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Build from foods that are already safe. One new food, or one new place to eat a safe food, is a full goal. Map fear: choking, vomiting, texture, novelty, or social exposure. Keep body weight out of motivational speeches.

### Family

Lower pressure at the table. One adult voice. Protect cultural foods and family meals even if the child's plate stays narrow. Ask who is exhausted and whether meals have destroyed dinner for everyone else.

### Parent training

Short meals, no force-feeding, praise for sitting or tasting if that is the step, and a planned end. Coach the adult who actually feeds the child. If a tube exists, follow the nutrition plan for what "success" means. Coming off a tube is not your unilateral goal.

## Intake questions

- What is eaten and drunk on a usual day, and what was cleared by speech or GI?
- Is there choking fear, vomiting fear, sensory refusal, low appetite, or a body-image drive? More than one can be true.
- What happens at the table in the ten minutes after a refusal?
- Which foods matter to this family's culture that a plan must not casually drop?
- Any autism, GI diagnosis, or tube?

## Differentials

- ARFID versus anorexia nervosa versus both. Ask about body image directly, in developmentally possible language.
- Food refusal versus pain, constipation, eosinophilic disease, or a swallowing problem.
- "Behavior at meals" versus a feeding skill delay or a sensory pattern OT already described.
- Cultural food practices versus a disorder. A narrow menu that meets nutrition and does not impair the child is not ARFID.

## Modifications

- Do not use snacks as rewards if food is the fear.
- Do not expand textures past the speech or GI clearance.
- Include the feeding therapist's current step so you do not contradict it.
- Use the feeding and cultural-eating training before you improvise a hierarchy.
