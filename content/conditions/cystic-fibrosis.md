## Overview

Cystic fibrosis is a genetic condition that affects the protein CFTR. Thick mucus, recurrent lung infections, pancreatic insufficiency, and high sweat salt are the classic picture. The liver, intestines, sinuses, bones, and reproductive tract can be involved. It is a different illness in different people, and a different illness than it was before CFTR modulators.

Highly effective modulator therapy, such as elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor for eligible mutations, has changed lung disease and daily life for many people. Not everyone is eligible. Access is uneven. A modulator does not erase the illness, the clinic, or the family's history of hospital time. Do not assume a family's burden matches a textbook from 2010, and do not assume the new medicine made therapy unnecessary.

CSC does not manage airway clearance, enzymes, or modulators. The CF team does. Your work is mood, anxiety, adherence conflict, eating, procedural fear, school, and the family system around a regimen that still takes time.

## Key treatments

Daily care often still includes airway clearance (a vest, manual percussion, or other techniques the respiratory therapist taught), inhaled medicines, and pancreatic enzymes with fat-containing meals and snacks. Doses are prescribed. You do not adjust enzyme capsules at the table.

Modulators are taken on the schedule the CF team set. Nutrition, including extra calories for some people and a different plan for others after modulators, is a dietitian's plan. Airway cultures, pulmonary function, and periodic admissions or tune-ups are medical care. CF-related diabetes is its own diabetes, not type 1 and not type 2. If it is present, read the diabetes card as well and let the CF and endocrine clinicians lead.

Some clinics still keep people with CF apart from each other because of germs that are hard to treat. Ask what this clinic does before you suggest a group. Peer support may be online or carefully arranged, not a waiting room full of other children with CF.

## Treatment burden

Even with modulators, many days include treatments that compete with school, sleep, and being a child. Vest time is boring. Enzymes at lunch are visible. Hospitalizations pull siblings and jobs out of shape. Caregivers become the treatment team at home, and then get criticized when a piece is skipped.

The burden is not the same in every house. A child on a highly effective modulator with mild lung disease is not living the same day as a child who is listed for transplant or who cannot take a modulator. Ask what this week actually contains before you import a generic chronic-illness script.

Weight has historically been a vital sign in CF. Comments about eating, from clinicians and from relatives, land differently than they do for a typical teenager. After modulators, some people gain weight they did not expect and feel judged in a new direction. Do not run a generic weight-loss frame. The dietitian and the CF team own the nutrition goal.

## Adherence

The pieces that slip are predictable: enzymes away from home, vest time when a friend is over, inhaled medicines that taste bad, refills that did not arrive, and modulator doses when the routine changed. Ask which piece, who notices, and what the fight sounds like.

Time and embarrassment are reasons. So are depression and a belief that the medicine stopped mattering because the child feels better. Feeling better is not the same as a plan to stop. Stopping is a CF-team decision.

Mood and sleep changes have been reported after starting or changing modulator therapy. Treat that report as information for the CF team. Do not blame the teenager, and do not tell the family to hold a dose. You also should not dismiss a new depression as "just the medicine" without the team and without your usual safety assessment.

Complete refusal of enzymes or airway clearance, or a secret stop, needs the CF team promptly. Shame makes families hide the stop. Make it easier to say.

## Mental health and development

The CF Foundation and the European Cystic Fibrosis Society recommend routine screening for depression and anxiety in people with CF and in caregivers. Elevated symptoms are common. Screening is a standard of CF care. A positive screen is the start of a conversation, not a label you assign alone.

Procedural anxiety, needle fear, cough, and hospital memories are frequent. So is caregiver traumatic stress after years of nights in the hospital. Siblings may be angry and guilty at the same time.

School absences, fatigue, and bathroom access affect learning and friends. Infection rules can make the child feel contagious or left out. Ask who they are allowed to see, and do not invent a playdate with another child with CF.

Most men with CF are infertile because the vas deferens did not form. That fact often arrives in adolescence, sometimes badly. It is a medical counseling topic. Do not be the person who blurts it out, and do not be surprised if it is already in the room unspoken. Questions about fertility, puberty, or sexual health go to the CF team, who can tell you what this young person has already been told. Women and girls with CF may have reduced fertility and can become pregnant. That counseling is also the CF team's.

Young children need caregivers to do the treatments. School-age children can own a piece of the routine and still need an adult for the parts that matter. Adolescents want the vest off and the enzymes in a pocket. Negotiate the visible pieces. Do not confuse a wish to be ordinary with a lack of understanding.

## Long-term effects

Lung disease can still progress. Some people need oxygen, noninvasive ventilation, or transplant evaluation. Advanced disease and survival uncertainty remain part of some families' lives in the modulator era. Do not reassure with a cure story.

CF-related diabetes, liver disease, bone disease, sinus disease, and distal intestinal obstruction syndrome are medical complications you should know exist so you do not psychologize a blockage or a new diabetes. Hearing can be affected by some antibiotic courses. Check what audiology has said before you interpret "not listening."

The long relationship with the CF team is itself a clinical fact. Families may trust that team more than a new therapist. Ask the team's formulation before you offer a competing one.

## Red flags

- Coughing blood, sudden breathlessness, chest pain, or a sharp drop in how the child usually breathes. Medical care now, not a coping skill.
- Fever or a change in cough or sputum the family was told to report.
- Severe abdominal pain, vomiting, or no stool, which can be a blockage. The CF team has a plan. You do not improvise laxatives.
- New or worsening depression, panic, or a clear change in mood or sleep after a modulator start or dose change. Tell the CF team. Do not stop the medicine yourself.
- Suicidal ideation, self-harm, or an eating pattern that has stopped enzymes, feeds, or most food.
- A complete stop of airway clearance or enzymes that the CF team does not know about.
- Any request for you to change a dose.

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Screen mood and anxiety the way the CF team expects, and tell the team what you find. Separate diabetes distress's cousin here: treatment burden, not a character flaw. Work on one skipped piece of the regimen.

Procedural and needle fear can be graded with the clinic's actual steps, not a generic fear ladder that skips the port or the culture. Body image and eating need the dietitian in the plan. Do not set a calorie goal.

If the child feels better on a modulator and wants to quit the rest, explore the wish and send the medical question back to clinic. Identity work matters: they are more than the vest, and the vest may still be on the list.

Give scripts for enzymes at the lunch table and for what to tell a new friend. Privacy is a reasonable goal.

### Family

Caregiver depression and anxiety are in scope for the CF mental-health model. Ask how the adult is sleeping. Map who does the evening treatment and what happens when that person is depleted.

Align adults so enzyme reminders are not a running criticism. Include siblings. Financial and time stress are part of the formulation, not small talk.

If infection rules have isolated the child, problem-solve contact that the clinic allows rather than pushing a group the clinic would forbid.

### Parent training

Build a treatment routine that can survive a weekday: same order, a visible end, a choice that is real (which show, not whether to do it). Praise the piece that happened.

For young children, the adult does the treatment. The child's job is a small cooperation skill. For school-age children, enzymes in the backpack are a rehearsed habit, with a school adult who knows. For adolescents, move from nagging to a check-in they helped write.

Do not punish coughs, low lung function, or weight. Those are medical data. Practice the family's rule for when to call the CF nurse. Do not write a stricter one to feel safe.

## Intake questions

- What is on today's regimen, and which piece is the one that actually gets skipped?
- Did mood, sleep, or anxiety change after a modulator was started or changed?
- How does eating and weight talk go in this family, and what has the dietitian asked for?
- What infection rules does this clinic use?
- Who is depleted: the child, a caregiver, or both?

## Differentials

- Low mood or irritability after a modulator change versus depression versus sleep loss. Report the timing to the CF team.
- Cough, chest pain, or breathlessness versus a panic attack. Pulmonary symptoms win until the CF team says otherwise.
- Restricted eating versus the nutrition plan versus an eating disorder versus cultural food practices. Do not collapse these.
- "Noncompliance" with enzymes versus not having them at school, embarrassment, or diarrhea the child is ashamed to describe.
- Inattention versus hearing loss after antibiotics, fatigue, or a busy lung day. Check audiology and sleep before an ADHD story hardens.
- CF-related diabetes symptoms versus a mood problem. If CFRD is present, highs and lows belong in the differential the way they do in type 1.

## Modifications

- Do not schedule across a treatment the child will then skip. Ask when the vest usually happens.
- Do not give enzyme, modulator, or diet advice.
- One regimen behavior on the goal list. Leave the rest for the CF team.
- Include the adult who does the evening treatment.
- If mood changed with a new modulator, document the timing in your note to the team and keep your own interpretation humble.
- Avoid a group intervention that mixes people with CF unless the clinic has said that is acceptable.
