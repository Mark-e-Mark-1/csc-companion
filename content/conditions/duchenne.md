## Overview

Stub for medical team review. Duchenne muscular dystrophy is a genetic condition, most often in boys, causing progressive muscle weakness. Heart and breathing muscles are involved over time. Learning, attention, autism, and emotional regulation problems are more common because dystrophin is also in the brain. Steroids are a disease-modifying treatment and a mood treatment problem. Newer genetic therapies exist for some mutations. The neuromuscular team decides.

Carrier mothers and sisters can have their own medical issues. Do not ignore a mother's report of weakness or heart symptoms. Send it to the team rather than turning it into a therapy theme only.

What the medical team still needs to add: your steroid regimen and sick-day plan, cardiac warning signs in therapist language, and which exon-skipping or gene therapies you do not want therapists explaining.

## Key treatments

Corticosteroids, cardiac medicines, respiratory care, PT and equipment, and mutation-specific therapies for some children. Steroid stress dosing on sick days is a medical plan. You do not give steroid advice. You need to know a plan exists so you do not tell a family to "just skip a dose" when the child is ill.

## Treatment burden

The care increases as walking, then transfers, then breathing change. Families are grieving in advance while also doing a hard daily routine. Steroids change appetite, sleep, and mood. Equipment and home modifications are a second job. Clinic days are long.

## Adherence

Steroids are hard because of mood and body changes, and stopping them suddenly can be dangerous. That risk is the team's to explain. Your job is the conflict and the side effects the family is afraid to report. Exercise recommendations are specific. "Try harder" is the wrong intervention.

## Mental health and development

ADHD, autism, anxiety, and learning disabilities are overrepresented. Assess them as part of Duchenne, not as a surprise. Steroid irritability can look like a behavior disorder. Disease progression brings grief that children understand earlier than adults wish. Use the developmental lens. A child can be gifted in one area and need support in another. Ahead and behind can both be true.

## Long-term effects

Loss of walking, contractures, heart disease, and respiratory failure shape the years. Timelines vary and have changed with steroids and newer care. Do not quote a life expectancy you remember from training. Heart and breathing symptoms are medical. Transition to adult neuromuscular care needs a plan.

## Red flags

- Chest pain, fainting, palpitations, or new trouble breathing. Emergency or neuromuscular team now.
- A family considering stopping steroids because of behavior. They need the neuromuscular clinician before they hold a dose. Sudden stops can be medically dangerous.
- Fractures after a fall. Steroids and weak bones make this more than a bruise.
- Suicidal ideation or a caregiver who cannot safely transfer.

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Mood on steroids, peer loss as mobility changes, and concrete coping for procedures. Learning and attention interventions should match the actual cognitive profile. Do not use walking as the measure of effort.

### Family

Anticipatory grief and caregiver injury are in scope. Parents may disagree about how much to tell the child. Help them align. Siblings, including carriers who are being tested, need their own sentence in the formulation.

### Parent training

Behavior support during steroid irritability: lower demands, protect sleep, do not start a new punishment system the week the dose changes. Keep one enjoyable activity that is physically possible this year. Ask PT what that is.

## Intake questions

- Where is mobility now, and what changed in the last year?
- What steroid is in use, and did mood or sleep change with it?
- Any cardiac or breathing symptoms the team has not heard?
- What learning, attention, or autism diagnoses are already documented?

## Differentials

- Steroid mood effects versus ADHD versus anxiety versus grief.
- Refusal to participate versus fatigue, pain, or a task that is no longer physically possible.
- Caregiver depression versus exhaustion from nighttime care.

## Modifications

- No physical icebreakers that ignore the PT precautions.
- Do not give steroid advice, including "take a break."
- Plan for fatigue. Shorter sessions are legitimate.
- Use the developmental-complexity training. A chronic-illness recording that covers Duchenne is not in the current map.
