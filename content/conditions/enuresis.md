## Overview

Stub for medical team review. Enuresis is repeated wetting, in the day, the night, or both, past the age when continence is expected. Nighttime wetting in a child who has never been dry is common and often maturational, with a genetic pattern. Secondary wetting, after a period of dryness, needs a look for medical and psychosocial causes. The scope lists enuresis under Path 1.

No recording in the current training map is specifically about enuresis. The encopresis material in the additional-topics set is adjacent and is not a substitute. Say that when you point an intern to it.

What the medical team still needs to add: your alarm protocol, your desmopressin rules, and when you treat constipation first.

## Key treatments

Treat constipation if it is present. A bedwetting alarm has a strong evidence base for nighttime wetting when the family can do it. Medicines such as desmopressin are a prescriber's tool for some children, especially for overnights away, and have a real safety rule about fluids. Scheduled voiding helps daytime wetting. You do not start the medicine or set the fluid rule.

## Treatment burden

Laundry, smell, a child who will not do sleepovers, and a parent who is up at night. Pull-ups in an older child are a dignity problem and sometimes a necessary bridge. The alarm disrupts the whole house.

## Adherence

Alarms fail when the child does not wake, the parent turns it off, or the family is too sleep-deprived to do a two-week trial. Punishment teaches hiding of sheets. Ask whether constipation treatment is actually happening. Wetting will not stop if the bowel is impacted.

## Mental health and development

Shame and social withdrawal are the usual effects, not the cause. A developmental difference can delay nighttime dryness and changes how you teach a voiding schedule. ADHD makes a daytime voiding schedule harder to remember. Abuse, trauma, and family stress belong in the differential for secondary enuresis. They are not the assumption for every wet bed.

## Long-term effects

Most nighttime wetting resolves. The harm that lasts is shame and family conflict if the years were punitive. Daytime wetting has a higher rate of co-occurring urinary and bowel problems and needs the medical plan followed through. Do not promise a date it will stop.

## Red flags

- Pain with urination, excessive thirst, weight loss, or new daytime accidents. Think infection, diabetes, or another medical cause. Pediatrics, not a reward chart, comes first.
- Secondary enuresis with mood change or a disclosure concern. Consider stressors and your reporting duty.
- Constipation with pain, vomiting, or no stool. Medical review.
- A medicine, including desmopressin, being used with extra fluids or in a way the prescriber did not describe. Send them back. Fluid overload is the risk you do not improvise around.
- Suicidal ideation or bullying about smell.

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Remove blame. For a school-age child, explain the bladder in one concrete picture. Social goals such as a sleepover come after a plan that works, not as a bribe. Include the child in choosing an alarm if one is prescribed. Do not make them describe the wet bed in detail for no reason.

### Family

The adult who is shaming needs a different job: laundry without commentary, or the alarm response. Align caregivers. A stepparent who wants punishment and a parent who wants to stop fighting is a common split.

### Parent training

If an alarm is the plan, rehearse the night routine: alarm, child to the bathroom, clean sheet strategy that is not a spectacle, back to sleep. No punishment. Daytime wetting gets scheduled voids and easy access, plus school help that is discreet. Treat constipation as part of the same plan if pediatrics said so.

## Intake questions

- Night, day, or both? Ever dry for six months or more?
- What has pediatrics or urology already done, including constipation, urinalysis, and any medicine?
- What do adults do when the bed is wet?
- Any developmental difference, ADHD, or a stressor that lines up with secondary wetting?

## Differentials

- Enuresis versus a urinary tract infection, diabetes, constipation, or a neurologic bladder. Medical screening is not optional at the start.
- Night wetting versus the child not wanting to leave the bed because of fear or because an adult responds with anger.
- Daytime urgency versus anxiety versus a voiding schedule the child cannot remember at their developmental level.

## Modifications

- Do not punish wet nights. Do not wake the child hourly as your own invention. If a night-waking plan exists, it should be the prescriber's.
- Know the fluid rule if desmopressin is in use, and do not design an exposure that breaks it.
- Match the alarm coaching to family sleep capacity. A plan no one can do for a week is not evidence the child is unmotivated.
- Be honest that the enuresis recording is not in the library yet.
