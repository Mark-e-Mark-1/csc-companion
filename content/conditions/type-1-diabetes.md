## Overview

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune loss of the cells that make insulin. The child needs insulin from outside the body to survive. It is not caused by eating sugar, and it is not type 2 diabetes. Families often have to say that out loud more than once, including to relatives and to school staff.

Care is a stream of decisions: dose, food, activity, illness, devices, and sleep. Endocrine owns the insulin plan, the glucose targets, and the technology. CSC's work is the behavioral and family side of living with that plan. You do not adjust doses, set a target range, or tell a family which pump settings to use.

Who is supposed to do which task changes with age. Distress often gets labeled noncompliance when it is fear, burnout, a skill the child does not have yet, or a number that was never going to be stable that day. Ask which task breaks down before you agree with the label.

## Key treatments

Insulin is given by injections or by a pump, on a plan the endocrine team writes. Some youth use a hybrid closed-loop system that adjusts insulin from a continuous glucose monitor. The system still needs a human. Sites fail, sensors fall off, and algorithms do not know about a soccer game or a skipped lunch.

Glucose is watched with a meter, a continuous glucose monitor, or both. Alarms, especially overnight, are part of the treatment and part of the exhaustion. Ketones are checked during illness or when glucose stays high, on the schedule the team taught the family.

Meals usually involve carbohydrate counting and a mealtime dose. Some younger children are on a more fixed food plan. Severe lows are treated with the fast sugar or glucagon product the team prescribed. You should know the family has a plan. You should not invent one.

School needs a written plan: who gives insulin, where supplies live, which staff are trained, and what happens on a field trip. If that plan is missing, the gap belongs on the problem list next to any behavior goal.

## Treatment burden

There is no day off. Meals, sports, sleepovers, exams, and sick days all need a plan. Night checks and alarms break sleep for the child and for the caregivers. Caregivers of young children carry the whole cognitive load. Adolescents may look independent on paper and still miss the lunch dose when no one is watching.

Technology reduces some of the work and adds alarms, skin problems, failed sites, and a device on a body that may not want to be seen. Insurance and supply orders are part of the illness. Siblings often get less attention. Conflict clusters around numbers, because the number is visible and the fear underneath it is not.

## Adherence

Adherence is a poor word for a task that is never finished. Ask which piece breaks down: the lunch bolus at school, wearing the sensor, checking ketones, guessing carbohydrates, or running high on purpose.

Diabetes distress is common. It is not the same thing as a primary depressive disorder, and both can be present. Fear of hypoglycemia leads some families to run high. That is a safety strategy with its own costs. Do not dismantle it without endocrine.

Insulin omission tied to weight or shape is a medical emergency pattern, seen more often in adolescence. It needs endocrine and, usually, an eating-disorder resource. It does not need a shame-based sticker chart. Using insulin to self-harm is a same-day safety issue.

Adolescents pull back from parent help before the skills are solid. Negotiate a specific handoff for one task this month. "You're old enough" is not a plan.

## Mental health and development

Depression, anxiety, and diabetes distress are more common than in peers. Screen for them. Do not assume every hard day is a psychiatric disorder, and do not assume it is "just diabetes" either.

Family conflict about glucose is one of the strongest behavioral patterns linked to trouble. Treat the conflict. A child who is scolded for every number learns to hide the number.

Needle fear and site-change fear are ordinary, especially early, and they are treatable with graded practice alongside the nursing plan. Young children cannot manage independently. A tantrum at a check may be developmental, plus pain, plus a frightened adult. School-age children can own a piece: wash hands, tell an adult they feel low, carry supplies. The adult still doses unless endocrine has said otherwise.

Teens are managing privacy, driving, sports, alcohol, dating, and the wish to be ordinary. Risk-taking can include silencing alarms or skipping insulin. Say that plainly, without a speech about future complications.

Hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia mimic mood and attention problems. A child who is irritable, slow, tearful, or "not listening" may be out of range. Ask what the glucose was at the time. Sleep loss from alarms mimics depression and ADHD.

## Long-term effects

Years of high glucose raise the risk of eye, kidney, and nerve complications. That conversation belongs to endocrine. Do not use complications as a threat in therapy. Severe hypoglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis are the acute dangers. Hypoglycemia unawareness can develop, which is why some families are so afraid of lows. Repeated severe lows and ketoacidosis, especially in young children, are also a reason the medical team thinks about learning and development. You support prevention. You do not deliver the prognosis talk.

Eating disorders and insulin omission drive both acute ketoacidosis and longer-term harm. Name the pattern early and bring endocrine in.

## Red flags

These are reasons to reach the medical team or emergency care, not topics to hold for next session.

- Signs of diabetic ketoacidosis: vomiting, stomach pain, fast or deep breathing, fruity breath, sleepiness, confusion. This is emergency care.
- Severe hypoglycemia: seizure, unconsciousness, or inability to swallow. Emergency care. Glucagon only as the family has already been taught.
- Insulin omission, running very high on purpose, or any use of insulin to self-harm.
- New disordered eating, or fear of insulin tied to weight.
- Suicidal ideation or self-harm of any kind, using your usual safety pathway and telling endocrine.
- A family so burned out that doses are not happening and endocrine does not know.
- Anyone asking you what number to bolus. Send them back to the on-call endocrine line. Do not guess.

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Name diabetes distress as a response to an unrelenting task. Map one breakdown, such as lunch doses at school, rather than a goal to "be more compliant."

If fear of lows is running the family's numbers, build a graded plan only inside the range endocrine has already set. Do not invent a glucose goal that sounds reasonable.

For body image and insulin omission, stay collaborative and non-shaming, and loop in the medical team early. Device wear is a practical problem: sports, skin, being seen. Rule out skin injury with nursing before you treat it as pure avoidance.

When mood is the referral, ask whether symptoms cluster with highs, lows, or alarm nights before you treat them as a separate psychiatric disorder. Offer concrete scripts for friends, coaches, and teachers. A teenager often needs the sentence more than the insight.

### Family

Move glucose out of a moral argument ("you don't care") and into a shared plan. Parents' fear is information. It is also exhausting to live with. Put the monitoring conflict on the table: what the caregiver checks, what the young person will show, and what happens when the number is bad.

Decide who does what this month and write it down, matched to developmental level. Include sibling and partner strain. Align the adults so the child does not live under two regimens.

### Parent training

Make checks and site changes short, predictable, and as calm as the day allows. Practice when no one is scared, not only in the crisis. Catch the child doing a skill and name that skill. Do not punish numbers. Numbers are data.

For young children, the caregiver does the task. The child's job is a small piece: choosing a finger, holding a supply, sitting. For teens, coach the parent from lecturing toward a pre-agreed check-in. Build a night plan that a human can keep. Ask the family to show you the endocrine rule for when to call, and practice that rule. Do not write a new one.

## Intake questions

- What was the glucose around the last few episodes of irritability, fatigue, or "not listening"?
- Who does which task this month: checks, doses, site changes, supplies, and the school plan?
- Where is the fight: numbers, food, devices, sleep, or independence?
- Any missed insulin, fear of weight gain, or hiding the number?
- What do alarms do to sleep for the child and the caregivers?

## Differentials

- Irritability, tearfulness, or aggression versus hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia. Get the glucose from that afternoon before you build a behavior plan.
- Inattention versus glucose variability versus sleep loss from alarms versus a separate ADHD diagnosis. The ADHD question can stay open. It should not be settled first.
- Depression versus diabetes distress versus burnout. They overlap and they are not identical.
- Restricted eating for weight, restricted eating from fear of carbohydrates and insulin, and ARFID-type restriction are different problems with different medical risk.
- Oppositional behavior versus a demand the child cannot yet do, such as counting carbohydrates or feeling a low in their body.

## Modifications

- A session during a low is not a therapy session. Keep the family's fast sugar in the room if they want it there, and follow their plan.
- Do not do fingersticks, and do not give insulin advice.
- One diabetes task at a time. Include the caregiver who actually does that task.
- For adolescents, split the hour: time alone and time together. Say the split out loud.
- If the late-day problem is the referral, ask them to bring the CGM or meter record, not only the story.
