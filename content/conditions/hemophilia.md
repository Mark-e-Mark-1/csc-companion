## Overview

Stub for medical team review. Hemophilia is an inherited bleeding disorder, most often factor VIII or IX deficiency. Severity varies. People of all sexes can be affected or be symptomatic carriers. Modern prophylaxis, including factor replacement and newer non-factor medicines, has changed joint disease for many children. The bleeding-disorders team owns the product, the dose, and activity limits.

What the medical team still needs to add: which products your clinic uses, your head-injury rule, inhibitor basics for therapists, and how you want activity restrictions explained.

## Key treatments

Regular prophylaxis to prevent bleeds, on-demand treatment for bleeds, and a written plan for procedures, dental work, and sports. Home infusion is a family skill. Physical therapy protects joints. Inhibitors, in a subset of children, make treatment much harder. Do not describe products you have not confirmed with the team.

## Treatment burden

Infusions, or the emotional load of a port, organize the week. Parents watch every limp. Activity decisions create conflict with coaches and relatives. The child may look fine and still be one injury away from a long bleed. Clinic visits and insurance for expensive medicines are their own job.

## Adherence

Prophylaxis slips when a child feels well, when a teen does not want to infuse, or when supplies fail. A missed dose is a bleeding-disorders conversation, not a character conversation. Ask what gets in the way of the specific dose.

## Mental health and development

Procedural fear, parent hypervigilance, and the child's wish to play the sport everyone else plays are the common themes. Young children cannot judge risk. School-age children can learn a simple report-the-limp rule. Adolescents hide bleeds and skip doses to be ordinary. Pain from a target joint affects mood and participation.

## Long-term effects

Joint disease, chronic pain, and, for some older regimens, historical infection risk that families may still mention. Inhibitors change the story. Transition to adult care is a known cliff. The team should say which of these apply to the child in front of you.

## Red flags

- Head, neck, belly, or eye injury, a severe headache after a hit, or a bleed that is not stopping. Follow the family's emergency plan. Do not watch a head injury to see if behavior improves.
- A joint that is swollen, warm, or refused. That can be a bleed, not defiance.
- Do not recommend ibuprofen, aspirin, or other medicines. The bleeding-disorders team has a list.
- Suicidal ideation, or a complete stop of prophylaxis the team does not know about.

## Therapy implications

### Individual

Fear of needles and fear of injury can be graded beside the real infusion steps. Help the child script how to tell a coach or a friend. Do not promise a sport the team has restricted.

### Family

Overprotection is often a successful safety strategy that has grown too wide. Shrink one limit at a time with the team's activity list in hand. Caregivers need a place to put the vigilance that is not the child's body.

### Parent training

Teach a calm injury script: stop, assess, call the number on the card. Praise reporting a bleed more than toughness. Match independence to age: a school-age child can carry the letter; a teen can own one piece of prophylaxis if the team agrees.

## Intake questions

- What product is in use, who infuses, and what is the head-injury rule?
- Which activities are allowed, limited, or off the list?
- Any target joint, inhibitor, or recent bleed that changed mood or school?
- Where do caregiver fear and the child's wish for independence collide?

## Differentials

- Refusal to walk or to participate versus a joint or muscle bleed.
- Anxiety versus appropriate caution after a real head-injury rule. Do not desensitize a family to the warning signs they were taught.
- Oppositional behavior in a teen versus skipped prophylaxis and fear of being seen infusing.

## Modifications

- Know the activity restriction before you assign an exposure, a sport metaphor, or a physical icebreaker.
- Do not comment on medicines.
- Include the adult who infuses.
- If the child is a girl or a symptomatic carrier, do not act surprised. Confirm the diagnosis with the team rather than guessing from an old stereotype.
